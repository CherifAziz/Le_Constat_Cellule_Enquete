Evaluating the eﬀects of the German debt brake:
A synthetic control approach†
Maximilian Langer1,2, Joshua Hassib1,2, Lars P. Feld1,2,*, Daniel Nientiedt1
1Walter Eucken Institute and 2University of Freiburg

Abstract
This paper assesses the eﬀectiveness and ﬁscal implications of the German debt brake, a
constitutional ﬁscal rule introduced in 2009 to ensure debt sustainability. To estimate its
causal eﬀects, we employ a synthetic control design that combines canonical, augmented,
and multiple-outcome synthetic control methods alongside comparative interrupted time-series
analysis. We compare the observed trajectories of six ﬁscal and macro-ﬁnancial outcome variables
in Germany with their counterfactual trajectories of a synthetic Germany without the rule. The
results indicate that the debt brake was a key driver of the consolidation of German public
ﬁnances during the 2010s. It substantially reduced debt accumulation and improved the primary
balance. By strengthening ﬁscal discipline, the rule likely also contributed to lower ﬁnancing
costs, although this eﬀect cannot be attributed to the debt brake exclusively. We ﬁnd no robust
evidence that the rule reduced public investment at the federal level. At the general government
level, investment eﬀects are less conclusive and may reﬂect vertical ﬁscal interactions across
diﬀerent levels of government. The results are supported by a range of inference procedures and
robustness checks.
Keywords: Fiscal rules, Fiscal federalism, German debt brake, Policy evaluation, Synthetic control
method

Introduction
In recent decades, there has been an increasing worldwide trend of introducing legal rules that
are intended to ensure sustainable public ﬁnances and increase the credibility of ﬁscal policy.
Provisions to restrict budget ﬂexibility are especially prevalent among OECD member states. With
the exception of Canada, all have ﬁscal rules in place since at least 2016 (Davoodi et al., 2022b).
Still, the proliferation of ﬁscal rules does not tell us anything about their eﬀectiveness in restricting
budgetary decisions. Past experience indicates that political actors will try to circumvent existing
regulations. Such structural deﬁciencies in ﬁscal institutions often result in large and persistent
deﬁcits. What matters is not only the quantity, but also the quality of ﬁscal rules (Reuter, 2019).
This paper assesses the eﬀectiveness of the German debt brake (Schuldenbremse) on several
macroeconomic variables. Introduced in 2009, this balanced budget rule enshrined in the German
constitution restricts annual structural deﬁcits to 0.35 percent of GDP at the federal level. It replaced
an earlier constitutional rule which had proven unable to halt the increasing trend in the German
debt-to-GDP ratio from the 1970s onward (Burret et al., 2013). Other factors in the decision to adopt
the debt brake were concerns about the implications of demographic change for public ﬁnances, as
well as debt crises that occurred in several German states (Länder).
Across diﬀerent countries, the literature shows that ﬁscal rules are associated with more
sustainable public ﬁnances, in particular lower public deﬁcits and lower interest rates on government
bonds (Heinemann et al., 2018). However, identifying the causal eﬀect of a given rule is diﬃcult, as
it requires considering a counterfactual situation in which the rule was not introduced in the ﬁrst
place. To estimate the causal eﬀect of the German debt brake, our paper employs the synthetic
control method (SCM), pioneered by Abadie and Gardeazabal (2003), Abadie et al. (2010), and
Abadie (2021). We create a synthetic Germany from a weighted average of other OECD countries.
The economic and ﬁnancial performance of the synthetic control closely mirrors the performance of
the real Germany, up to the introduction of the debt brake. Comparing the synthetic Germany’s
further development to the development of the real Germany after its introduction of the debt brake
allows us to estimate the causal eﬀect of this rule. We are especially interested in the trajectory of
six outcome variables, the debt-to-GDP ratio (general government and federal level), the general
government primary balance, the 10-year government bond interest rate, and the public investment
rate (general government and federal level). Brieﬂy, our empirical analysis suggests that the debt
brake was a major driver of the consolidation of German public ﬁnances during the 2010s.
A central empirical challenge in evaluating ﬁscal rules is endogeneity: jurisdictions may adopt
a rule because they are already on a consolidation path (selection on trends), adopt it in
response to adverse ﬁscal shocks (reverse causality), or do so amid latent political preferences
that simultaneously shape both the decision to adopt and subsequent outcomes. In such cases,
comparisons risk attributing to the rule what would have occurred anyway, or what is jointly
driven by unobserved, time-varying determinants, such as shifts in ﬁscal preferences, enforcement
intensity, or market discipline. These mechanisms imply correlation between the treatment and

the error term, threatening causal interpretation. Our design addresses these concerns along
two dimensions: institutionally, the reform’s timing and legal rigidity constrain short-run policy
discretion; methodologically, we employ canonical, augmented, and multiple-outcome synthetic
control approaches to recover counterfactual trajectories that match pre-treatment dynamics and
thereby account for observed and unobserved drivers insofar as they are reﬂected in those dynamics.
Given the institutional features of the debt brake (see Section 2)—notably constitutional
entrenchment, a legally ﬁxed date at which the rule became binding in 2011, and limited
short-run discretion—the rule’s binding implementation can be regarded as quasi-exogenous to
contemporaneous ﬁscal behavior. Enacted by constitutional amendment in 2009 but binding for
the federal budget only from 2011 onward, with a transition phase requiring the government to
gradually reduce its structural net borrowing until 2016, the reform’s timing and content were
predetermined rather than chosen in response to immediate ﬁscal conditions or short-term shifts in
policy preferences. In this sense, the rule functioned as an externally imposed constraint with a ﬁxed
timeline: policymakers initially had little discretion to alter course, and the rule took eﬀect largely
independently of short-term maneuvering. These features give the intervention a quasi-experimental
character.
Political preferences may have made the constitutional amendment feasible in 2009, but they
do not imply that identical outcomes would have emerged absent the rule. Once codiﬁed, the
rule’s binding onset and stringency were not dimensions that short-run preferences could easily
inﬂuence. The timing of the eﬀects further speaks against a purely preference-based explanation.
The debt brake was adopted in 2009 only months before a federal election, so the preferences
that made the constitutional amendment possible are not necessarily identical to the ﬁscal-policy
preferences of the governments responsible for budgetary decisions during most of the post-adoption
period. Moreover, even if one assumes that consolidation preferences persisted after 2009, a purely
preference-based explanation would imply that the divergence between Germany and its synthetic
control should already emerge around the adoption of the rule or shortly thereafter. Instead, for most
ﬁscal outcomes, the main adjustment patterns appear after the debt brake became binding in 2011
and during the subsequent transition period. This timing is more consistent with the debt brake
operating as an institutional constraint than with an explanation based solely on ﬁscal preferences,
although it cannot fully rule out a role for such preferences.
The ﬁscal-policy shift following the 2025 reform is also consistent with this interpretation:
once the ﬁscal framework was loosened, the federal government moved toward substantially higher
borrowing. This ex-post development is not part of our identiﬁcation strategy, but it supports the
view that the rule had previously operated as a binding constraint. Consistent with this view, our
empirical strategy treats 2011 as a quasi-exogenous treatment and uses the SCM to match pre-2011
trajectories.
Even if one does believe that underlying ﬁscal preferences or macroeconomic conditions did
coincide with the rule’s introduction, the SCM mitigates endogeneity concerns by accounting for
both observed and unobserved pre-treatment factors in constructing the counterfactual. Since its
2

==== page 6 ====
introduction by Abadie and Gardeazabal (2003), SCM has relied on weighting control units to
reproduce the treated unit’s pre-intervention outcomes. This procedure eﬀectively controls for
time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity (such as underlying policy preferences or other latent
traits) that might otherwise bias the estimates. In fact, Abadie et al. (2010) note that with a
suﬃciently long pre-intervention history, matching on pre-treatment outcomes can “allow us to
control for the heterogeneous responses to multiple unobserved factors,” because only units similar in
both observables and unobservables would exhibit comparable pre-treatment trajectories. Moreover,
unlike standard diﬀerence-in-diﬀerences approaches (which assume any unobserved confounders
have constant eﬀects over time), the SCM’s linear factor model allows the inﬂuence of unobserved
variables to vary with time (Abadie, 2021). This ﬂexibility makes endogeneity less severe than in
conventional comparative designs, although it does not eliminate the need for careful robustness
checks.
Theoretically, the introduction and enforcement of ﬁscal rules are closely linked to the interaction
between the dynamics of public debt, macroeconomic stability, and the political economy of
budgetary decisions. Critics of ﬁscal constraints often argue that they limit the government’s
capacity for countercyclical ﬁscal policy, potentially exacerbating macroeconomic volatility (Fatáş
and Mihov, 2006). Without such constraints, it is theoretically conceivable that governments could
stabilize economic cycles more eﬀectively through expansionary ﬁscal measures. However, the
eﬀectiveness of ﬁscal rules heavily depends on their design, enforcement, and the level of government
at which they are implemented (Schaechter et al., 2012; Sutherland et al., 2005). Indeed, mere de
jure enactment of ﬁscal rules does not guarantee de facto compliance or eﬀectiveness (Davoodi et al.,
2022a; Eyraud et al., 2017; Reuter, 2019). Moreover, compliance with supranational frameworks has
proven challenging, as indicated by the limited adherence to the European Stability and Growth
Pact (SGP) (Larch et al., 2023).
Relatedly, Grembi et al. (2016), exploiting a quasi-experimental setting in Italy, show that
relaxing previously imposed subnational ﬁscal rules led to higher deﬁcits and lower tax revenues
among aﬀected municipalities. Their ﬁndings underscore that not only the existence of ﬁscal rules,
but also their strict enforcement play a crucial role in maintaining ﬁscal discipline. In practice,
governments often fail to conduct stabilizing, anticyclical policies in the absence of ﬁscal rules
(Potrafke, 2023), and the introduction of these rules can even enhance anticyclical behavior
(Larch et al., 2021). Reuter et al. (2022) further report reduced ﬁscal volatility under ﬁscal
constraints. By mitigating unexpected policy changes and political conﬂicts over public ﬁnances
(Fernández-Villaverde et al., 2015), well-designed and credibly enforced rules can thus improve
macroeconomic performance.
A further dimension to the theoretical argument relates to the sustainability of debt and its
implications for economic growth. Numerous studies have documented a relationship between
high public debt levels and diminished long-term growth prospects (Reinhart and Rogoﬀ, 2010;
Afonso and Jalles, 2013). In particular, Reinhart et al. (2012) demonstrate that historically, high
debt-to-GDP ratios (exceeding 90%) tend to be associated with lower growth rates over prolonged
3

==== page 7 ====
periods. While endogeneity issues persist, cumulative evidence suggests that high debt levels
can impose a tangible drag on economic performance. Limiting the scope for unchecked debt
accumulation through credible ﬁscal rules can help curb these adverse growth eﬀects. For instance,
Berg et al. (2018) and Gründler and Scheuermeyer (2018) ﬁnd that constraining the potential
for public spending expansions may prevent or mitigate the negative consequences of large public
sectors on growth. Moreover, sustainable ﬁscal policies may not only avert negative growth outcomes
but may even foster stronger growth (Gründler and Potrafke, 2020b). In this regard, Fuest et al.
(2019) underscore that credible ﬁscal constraints, such as the German debt brake, serve to maintain
conﬁdence in public ﬁnances and may yield long-term economic beneﬁts.
Central to the debate on ﬁscal constraints is their role in ensuring long-term ﬁscal sustainability.
Debt sustainability hinges on maintaining debt ratios below thresholds at which conventional
funding—through taxation or stable bond markets—ceases to be feasible (Davig et al., 2011).
The theoretical benchmark is the intertemporal budget constraint, under which today’s debt must
equal the net present value of future primary surpluses. Here, the relationship between the interest
rate r and the GDP growth rate g (the r-g diﬀerential) is critical. In this context, it is crucial
to highlight that deﬁcits and debt levels directly aﬀect government borrowing costs and interest
rates. Engen and Hubbard (2004) note they may rise in response to expanding government debt,
investment can be crowded out, interest-sensitive purchases of durable goods may decline, and the
value of households’ assets can fall, thereby indirectly reducing consumption via wealth eﬀects. After
controlling for macroeconomic conditions and using a consistent data framework, they ﬁnd that an
increase in federal government debt equivalent to 1% of GDP would generally raise the long-term
real interest rate by about three basis points. Laubach (2009) provides evidence that a 1 percentage
point rise in the projected deﬁcit-to-GDP ratio increases sovereign bond yields by about 25 basis
points, while a comparable rise in the debt-to-GDP ratio adds roughly 3 to 4 basis points. Wachtel
and Young (1987) show that unexpected announcements of higher deﬁcits rapidly translate into
higher yields, reﬂecting markets’ sensitivity to ﬁscal imbalances. At the same time, investors value
the safety and liquidity of sovereign debt (Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen, 2012), such that
credible ﬁscal policies and rules can enhance demand for government bonds, lowering spreads and
borrowing costs. Indeed, studies ﬁnd that adopting and adhering to ﬁscal rules reduces sovereign
risk premia (Feld et al., 2017; Thornton and Vasilakis, 2018), an eﬀect that can be substantial and
more pronounced in ﬁscally responsible environments or during economic downturns (Afonso and
Jalles, 2019). Within monetary unions, high-debt countries’ ﬁscal stances can also inﬂuence not just
domestic but area-wide interest rates (Faini, 2006).
The eﬀect of ﬁscal rules on public investment is more complex and less conclusively determined
by theoretical and empirical studies. Critics often contend that strict ﬁscal rules cause governments
to cut back on capital expenditures, especially under binding constraints during downturns, due to
political incentives favoring immediate, visible spending over long-term investment (Bouton et al.,
2020; Ardanaz and Izquierdo, 2022). However, subnational ﬁscal rules, if thoughtfully designed, can
help manage local-level spending pressures without necessarily undermining investment (Sutherland
4

==== page 8 ====
et al., 2005; Schaechter et al., 2012). Empirically, the record is mixed. While some studies ﬁnd no
signiﬁcant relationship between ﬁscal rules and public investment (Potrafke, 2023; Salvi et al.,
2020; Vinturis, 2023), others identify positive associations (Burret and Feld, 2018a; Gregori, 2014;
Dahan and Strawczynski, 2013) or negative eﬀects (De Biase and Dougherty, 2022; Jürgens, 2022;
Venturini, 2020). A meta-analysis by Blesse et al. (2023) underscores this ambiguity, suggesting
that the impact depends on the rule’s design, ﬂexibility, and the broader institutional setting. The
German debt brake exempliﬁes these complexities, with debates on whether to exempt certain
types of investments (Fuest et al., 2019). International surveys among economists further conﬁrm
the contentious nature of these eﬀects (Gründler and Potrafke, 2020a). Beyond these considerations,
vertical ﬁscal eﬀects warrant attention. Such eﬀects can arise if subnational governments engage in
unsustainable expenditure growth under the assumption that higher federal levels will ultimately
bear the associated costs (Burret and Feld, 2018a; Rodden, 2002; Von Hagen et al., 2000).
In addition, political economy arguments provide a crucial rationale for implementing ﬁscal
rules. Kydland and Prescott’s (1977) foundational concept of time inconsistency highlights that
policies deemed optimal ex ante may be abandoned ex post due to short-term pressures, leading to
welfare losses. Politicians facing electoral cycles or inﬂuenced by interest groups may be tempted
to pursue short-sighted ﬁscal expansions, resulting in persistent deﬁcits and rising debt stocks.
Without constraints, even well-intentioned governments may struggle to resist these opportunistic
spending pressures. Fiscal rules serve as a commitment device, limiting the capacity of policymakers
to manipulate budgetary decisions for electoral gains or to cater to narrow special interests. This
includes mitigating the “common-pool” problem in public ﬁnance, according to which various
stakeholder groups draw on a shared resource—the public budget—without internalizing the full
cost of their demands (Schaltegger and Feld, 2009; Egger and Koethenbuerger, 2010; Fritz and
Feld, 2020). By imposing credible, transparent limits, ﬁscal rules encourage governments to set
priorities, align policies with the broader public interest, and maintain a sustainable ﬁscal stance
that ultimately beneﬁts both current and future generations. Such rules can also help stabilize
long-term planning and policy implementation as well as reinforce the credibility of no-bailout
commitments, preventing strategic debt accumulation aimed at constraining successor governments
(Gootjes et al., 2021).
In sum, these ﬁndings suggest that sound ﬁscal frameworks, including well-designed and credibly
enforced ﬁscal rules, contribute to macroeconomic stability, improve growth prospects, contain
sovereign risk, and lower borrowing costs. Although implications for public investment, vertical ﬁscal
spillovers, and interest rate spreads remain contingent on rule design, enforcement, and institutional
contexts, the evidence largely points to the beneﬁts of disciplined ﬁscal governance.
Our study contributes to this current state of research in several ways. First, we conﬁrm the
eﬀects generally attributed to ﬁscal rules in the existing literature. Speciﬁcally, we show that a
stringent ﬁscal rule can lead to a sustained reduction in debt levels and has a positive impact on the
primary balance. Furthermore, we ﬁnd no inﬂuence of ﬁscal rules on the level of public investment, at
least robustly at the federal government level. This ﬁnding is consistent with the majority of existing
5

==== page 9 ====
studies. A negative impact on long-term ﬁnancing costs is likely, although we cannot attribute this
eﬀect to the debt brake exclusively.
Second, we address vertical eﬀects of ﬁscal rules. Theoretical considerations suggest such eﬀects,
but empirical investigations on this topic remain scarce. Our analysis indicates a negative eﬀect on
subnational debt accumulation. Furthermore, we cannot fully rule out eﬀects on subnational public
investment activities though the eﬀects are not robust. This outcome has design implications for
ﬁscal rules: even if the desired eﬀects occur at the level where a rule is implemented, other federal
levels may also be aﬀected and must therefore be taken into account. In Germany, the debt brake
applies to the federal government and the states, but not directly to municipalities.
Our third contribution is methodological. Inference is a current topic in the SCM literature.
Therefore, we implement three diﬀerent inference procedures to strengthen the credibility of our
results. Additionally, we introduce a novel approach by calculating joint weights for multiple outcome
variables and augment these weights to further reduce bias.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, we brieﬂy describe the
institutional setting of the German debt brake and its technical properties as well as the development
of OECD and EU ﬁscal rules. Section 3 presents our empirical approach, including an overview of
the SCM, the inference approaches, and the data. Our main ﬁndings on the eﬀect of the debt rule on
aggregate indicators are reported in Section 4. Section 5 includes robustness checks and a detailed
discussion of our ﬁndings. Finally, Section 6 concludes.

Institutional setting
2.1
German Debt Brake
The introduction of the debt brake resulted from a long trend of an increasing debt ratio in Germany
since the 1970s. Oil price induced recessions in 1973 and 1979/80 as well as German reuniﬁcation in
1990 caused a signiﬁcant increase of public debt at both federal and state levels.1 Ongoing attempts
to dampen this trend failed, even in economically prosperous periods. Moreover, the imprecise
deﬁnition of public investment as well as the lack of sanctioning instruments lead to the ongoing
exploitation of public means, resulting in a trend of a steadily rising debt ratio in Germany (Burret
et al., 2013; Feld et al., 2019). These ﬁndings, combined with record levels of debt amounting to
more than 80 percent of GDP in the aftermath of the ﬁnancial crisis in 2008, ultimately resulted in
the anchoring of a fundamentally reformed ﬁscal rule in the German constitution.
In 2009, the debt brake was adopted and incorporated into constitutional law. Central to this
are Articles 109 and 115 of the German Basic Law, which have required the federal and state
governments to maintain a structurally balanced budget. Structural, i.e., cyclically adjusted, new
borrowing was limited to 0.0 percent for the states and a maximum of 0.35 percent of nominal GDP
for the federal government. The regulations became binding to the federal government for the ﬁrst

did not apply until 2016. During this transition phase, the 2010 structural deﬁcit had to be reduced
in equal steps. For the states, the prohibition of structural deﬁcits was in eﬀect since the ﬁscal year
2020 until 2024, though the states used escape clauses until at least 2023 because of the COVID-19
pandemic. Due to the challenges in deﬁning public investment2, the new regulation abandons the
concept of limiting borrowing to the level of investment. Since 2025, the states are allowed to run a
structural deﬁcit of up to 0.35 percent. Moreover, defense expenditures exceeding 1 percent of GDP
are exempted from the debt brake.
The federal government has been allowed to exceed the borrowing limit of 0.35% of GDP in two
cases. The ﬁrst exception takes economic conditions into account. The ﬁnancial leeway of 0.35% is
expanded by the cyclical adjustment component (CAB), which enables symmetric consideration of
economic business cycles. During periods of economic downturn, borrowing capacities are expanded,
while they are restricted during economically favorable times. Higher public debt incurred during
economic slowdowns must subsequently be reduced. The second case in which the borrowing limit
of 0.35% is allowed to be exceeded is ‘in the event of natural disasters or extraordinary emergencies
beyond the control of the state that signiﬁcantly aﬀect the ﬁnancial position of the state’ (Article 115
(2) of the German Constitution), hereafter called “exemption clause”. Such decisions must also be
accompanied by a repayment plan.3 Only since 2025, defense spending provides another exception.
Another key criticism of earlier versions of the ﬁscal rules was their focus on borrowing limits
based on the investment expenditures planned in the budget, rather than on actual budget execution.
The debt brake addresses this issue through the introduction of a control account. This account
records non-cyclical positive and negative deviations from the ceiling on the federal government’s
structural net borrowing. If the cumulative negative balance of the account exceeds 1.5 percent
of GDP, this excess must be reduced in a way consistent with economic conditions within a
reasonable period. This design extends the oversight of the debt brake to both budget preparation
and execution, eﬀectively closing the previously described loophole to circumvent the debt brake.
Until 2010, special funds (Sondervermögen) were not included in the oﬃcial budget and could be
managed as oﬀ-budget entities with their own borrowing authorizations. As of 2011, special funds
have been subject to the regulations of the debt brake. However, these rules can be suspended
through an amendment to the Basic Law. This option was used for the €100 billion special fund
for defense in 2022 and for the €500 billion special fund for infrastructure and climate protection
in 2025.
Since its implementation in 2011, several criticisms of the framework of the debt brake
have emerged. First, the debt brake still allows for signiﬁcant room for maneuver. Second, its
implementation at the state level can be described as at least partially insuﬃcient. Third, within
the federal structure of government, there are insuﬃcient incentives to comply with the debt brake,
which makes vertical eﬀects conceivable (Burret et al., 2013; Kirchgässner, 2017). Although the

public debate has primarily centered on reform options aimed at softening the existing rules.
At the heart of the criticism lies the exclusion of the ‘Golden Rule’ by the debt brake: Since
its introduction, public investment expenditures can no longer be exempted from the borrowing
restrictions. The primary argument is that necessary public investments are being neglected. This
regulation is considered particularly problematic when the interest rate on government bonds is
persistently lower than the growth rate of nominal GDP. Furthermore, critics argue that the debt
brake restricts the ﬁscal space for countercyclical ﬁscal policy, limiting the government’s ability to
respond appropriately during economic downturns.
Other points of criticism include the long-term convergence of the debt-to-GDP ratio toward
excessively low levels under a balanced budget and the shift to implicit debt through future
obligations, particularly in the context of social insurance systems. Furthermore, the observed
recovery of public ﬁnances following record borrowing during the ﬁnancial crisis is not attributed to
the debt brake, but rather to the generally favorable economic environment, which led to declining
government expenditures and rising revenues.
2.2
European and OECD Fiscal Rules
Since the beginning of the 1990s, there has been a steady rise in the number of ﬁscal rules. Since
2016, every OECD member country (with the exception of Canada) has introduced at least one
kind of ﬁscal rule, since 2017 every one of these countries has implemented either a national ﬁscal
rule or both, a national and a supranational rule (Davoodi et al., 2022a; Larch et al., 2023).4
Sharp increases in the number of countries with ﬁscal rules within the OECD consistently
coincide with the introduction of EU policies aimed at ensuring ﬁscal stability within the framework
of the European Economic and Monetary Union (EMU). The member states of the EU agreed in the
1992 Maastricht Treaty on convergence criteria that countries must meet to join EMU. To ensure
ﬁscal stability, two of these criteria (a deﬁcit-to-GDP ratio below 3% of GDP and a debt-to-GDP
ratio below 60%) were enshrined in the Stability and Growth Pact (SGP) in 1997, extending their
applicability to all EMU member states beyond Euro accession. In addition, sanctioning mechanisms
were introduced to address noncompliance with the rules.
The SGP has been reformed regularly since then, mainly because the member states did not
comply. The ﬁrst reform of the SGP took place in 2005, resulting in a signiﬁcant relaxation of its
provisions. Following a sharp increase in debt ratios due to the ﬁnancial crisis, the SGP underwent
a second reform after 2011 to strengthen sanctioning mechanisms and enhance the enforcement of
its rules. The most recent reform of the SGP occurred in 2024, introducing greater ﬂexibility and
customization to align the enforcement of the Maastricht criteria with the speciﬁc circumstances of
individual member states.
At the beginning of 2012, the Treaty on Stability, Coordination, and Governance in the Economic
and Monetary Union (commonly known as the ‘Fiscal Compact’) was adopted to strengthen the

3.2
Comparative Interrupted Time Series
Typically, Interrupted Time Series Analysis (ITS) is applied in the same setting as the SCM. Both
methods aim to assess the eﬀects of an intervention, usually on a large scale, and in both cases the
eﬀective sample size is equal to 1. Both approaches are popular evaluation methods in cases where
a randomized controlled trial is not feasible (Linden and Adams, 2011; Linden, 2018).
In its most basic conﬁguration, the ITS compares the changes in the level and slope of the
parameters after an intervention. Even in the single case, when no comparison group is available,
ITS can produce valid results, although there is a signiﬁcant risk of misleading results when not
conducted carefully (Baicker and Svoronos, 2019). In contrast, comparative interrupted time series
(CITS) oﬀers higher validity when there is a plausible counterfactual that is similar to the treated
unit at both the baseline level and the trend of the outcome variables in the pre-treatment period.
Thus, CITS allows us to evaluate the SCM in a familiar regression framework. It also allows to
assess level and slope eﬀects, both after the introduction and after the suspension of the debt brake
in 2020. The CITS approach assumes the following model (Linden and Adams, 2011):
Yt = β0 + β1Tt + β2Xt + β3XtTt + β4Z + β5ZTt + β6ZXt + β7ZXtTt + ϵt
(6)
Yt is the outcome variable, Tt is the time since the beginning of the observation period, Xt is a dummy
variable that is 0 in the pre-treatment period and 1 in the post-treatment period. Z is a dummy
variable that takes the value 1 for the treated unit and 0 for the control unit. The parameters β0, β1,
β2, and β3 refer to the control group and represent the change in the intercept (or level), the slope (or
trajectory) until the intervention, the change in the intercept and the slope immediately following
the intervention, respectively. β4 and β5 refer to the treated unit and represent the diﬀerence in
the intercept between the treated and the control unit and the diﬀerence in the slope between the
treated and the control unit during the pre-treatment period. β6 reﬂects the diﬀerence between the
treated unit and its synthetic control in the immediate level of the outcome variable following the
intervention. β7 captures the diﬀerence in the slope of the outcome variable between the two groups
after the intervention, compared to the period before the intervention (diﬀerence in diﬀerence in
11

==== page 15 ====
slopes).
Intuitively, β4 and β5 indicate the quality of the pre-treatment ﬁt at both the level and the
trajectory of the outcome variable, while the eﬀects of the intervention are evaluated by changes
in the level and slope of the parameters β6 and β7. These parameters also allow us to assess the
statistical signiﬁcance of the intervention. Furthermore, these coeﬃcients allow us to calculate the
slope of the control in the post-intervention period (β1 + β3), the slope of the treated unit in the
post-intervention period (β1 + β3 + β5 + β7) and the diﬀerence between the treated unit and its
synthetic control in the post-intervention slope (β5 + β7). Finally, CITS allows us to test for more
than one intervention. Because the escape clause of the debt brake was activated from 2020-2023
due to COVID-19, this feature allows us to evaluate the eﬀects of this suspension. The activation
can be considered as exogenous, because COVID-19 can be considered as an external shock that
aﬀected Germany and its synthetic control equally. In contrast, the implementation in 2011 may
suﬀer from endogeneity: It is possible that the debt brake was installed due to the preferences of
the then existing government. The eﬀects of the debt brake, e.g., a decreasing debt ratio, may have
been due to the government conviction to run a sustainable budget rather than the restrictions
imposed by the debt brake. However, as noted by Abadie and Gardeazabal (2003), Abadie et al.
(2010), Abadie (2021), Eliason and Lutz (2018), and Herrero-Alcalde et al. (2024), the potential
endogeneity arising from pre-existing preferences for ﬁscal discipline does not pose a fundamental
problem in the context of SCM. Unlike traditional econometric approaches, SCM eﬀectively accounts
for both observed and unobserved confounders.
Following a linear factor model framework (e.g., Abadie (2021)), the outcome variable can
be expressed as Yjt = δt + θtZj + λtµj + ϵjt where δt represents a common time trend, Zj and
µj are vectors of observed and unobserved predictors, respectively, with coeﬃcients θt and λt,
and ϵjt is an idiosyncratic shock. Unlike diﬀerence-in-diﬀerences models, which restrict λt to be
time-invariant, SCM allows λt to vary over time, capturing dynamic unobserved heterogeneity. In
this context, if ﬁscal preferences inﬂuenced both (1) the variables of interest, e.g., the debt ratio,
and (2) the adoption of a ﬁscal rule, the SCM would still provide an appropriate counterfactual
by ensuring that the synthetic control unit exhibits a similar pre-treatment debt trajectory. If the
observed post-treatment divergence were driven by unobserved trends—such as a broader shift in
ﬁscal preferences—placebo tests across untreated units would reveal comparable patterns, thereby
invalidating a causal interpretation of the ﬁscal rule. Furthermore, the inclusion of donor pool units
with similar ﬁscal orientations enhances comparability, reducing bias from latent confounders. Thus,
SCM inherently mitigates endogeneity concerns by modeling time-varying unobserved factors and
ensuring that any observed treatment eﬀect is not confounded by pre-existing ﬁscal preferences.

3.4
Data and donor pool
To ensure the validity of the SCM approach, the choice of the control group is essential. The period
under review is 1995-2022. First, the 38 members of the OECD are considered. These countries
have structural similarities with Germany and are subject to similar economic developments. Due
to insuﬃcient data availability and comparability, the countries Chile (joined in 2010), Colombia
(joined in 2020), and Costa Rica (joined in 2021) are excluded from the analysis. In addition,
countries that experienced a national debt crisis during the observation period, as deﬁned by Nguyen
et al. (2022), are excluded. This applies to Costa Rica (1981-1996), Greece (2012-2013, 2018), Ireland
(2013), Mexico (1982-1990), Portugal (1999), and Turkey (1999). In the baseline analysis, the donor
pool thus consists of 18 to 27 countries, depending on data availability. Finally, the countries in the
control group should not be subject to ﬁscal rules that limit the ﬁscal ﬂexibility of the public sector.
However, as Figure 14 shows, almost every country in the OECD is subject to some kind of ﬁscal
rule. More important than being subject to some ﬁscal rule is the quality of this rule. The mere
existence of a ﬁscal rule does not say anything about the eﬀectiveness (see Figure 24 in Appendix
II).
To create a valid counterfactual, it is necessary only to exclude countries that have more credible
ﬁscal rules than the treated unit. We will address this issue by excluding all non-EU countries and
all EU countries that show equal or stronger compliance with the Maastricht criteria than Germany
during the period of validity of the German debt brake (2011-2019) according to the work of Larch
et al. (2023). It should be noted that even if ﬁscal rules were to signiﬁcantly aﬀect an outcome
variable in one of the control states for which a positive weight in the synthetic control is assigned,
this is likely to attenuate the estimated eﬀect for Germany at best, at least in the cases where the
direction of the eﬀect is known ex ante (which is true for all outcome variables except the investment

Primary Balance: General Government
The relationship between the primary balance and the debt ratio is central to ﬁscal stability and
debt dynamics in a country. A sustainable, positive primary balance can help reduce or stabilize
the debt ratio, which strengthens ﬁscal capacity and, in the long term, reduces debt servicing costs.
However, a high debt level can increase the pressure to generate primary surpluses to maintain a
stable debt ratio, especially when economic growth is low. A null eﬀect on the primary balance
would suggest that reductions in the debt ratio are primarily due to positive economic conditions
and historically low interest rates, rather than the result of primary surpluses. The sustainability of
public debt is largely dependent on the level of the primary government balance.

synthetic control group (see Figure 1 in section 4.2). In 2020, a sharp drop in the primary balance
is observed, surpassing the decline seen in the synthetic control group, resulting in a negative gap.

4.3
Vertical Eﬀects
The German debt brake has been eﬀective at the federal level since 2011. At the state level, the
introduction of a debt brake became mandatory starting in 2020; however, the states used the
escape clause since that year due to the COVID-19 pandemic. During the observation period, the
debt brake was fully applicable at the federal level from 2011 to 2019. Local jurisdictions are not
directly aﬀected by the debt brake.
As a federal state, there are numerous connections between the federal government, the states,
and the municipalities (Bury and Feld, 2025). First, there are extensive ﬁscal interactions. As they
are legally ﬁxed, increasing or decreasing transfers between diﬀerent layers of government is diﬃcult.
Second, the federal and state governments can impose spending restrictions onto local jurisdictions.
In light of these extensive interconnections, vertical ﬁscal eﬀects, such as an unsustainable
expansion of expenditures driven by the awareness that the resulting costs can be externalized
23

==== page 27 ====
to higher or lower federal levels, are conceivable (Rodden, 2002; Von Hagen et al., 2000). Direct
ﬁnancial constraints imposed by the federal government in the form of reduced transfers or delegation
of responsibilities are not necessarily required. If the federal government has a suﬃciently strong
reputation for ﬁscal discipline, ﬁscal rules will lead to lower borrowing by local government, because
the federal government can credibly commit to not transferring resources to indebted lower federal
tiers. However, empirical investigations on vertical eﬀects are scarce. We are aware of only one
empirical publication that ﬁnds no vertical eﬀect of ﬁscal rules at a higher federal level (Burret and
Feld, 2018b).

If the observed eﬀects were attributable to the debt brake, this would most likely be due to
task reassignments or declining transfers from the federal and state governments. Under the 2009

trend in the gap plot afterwards, starting in 2011. From 2012 onward, the federal government
introduced substantial social policy relief for municipalities, and beginning in 2015, their share of
VAT revenue was increased. At the same time, however, municipal responsibilities—particularly
in the social sector—were expanded following a practice with much tradition before and after the
introduction of the debt brake (Rietzler, 2022).
Between 2016 and 2021, investment-related transfers to municipalities rose by 60%, twice the rate
of non-investment-related transfers (Christofzik, 2023). If the debt brake led to reduced municipal
investment, this would likely reﬂect indirect consolidation pressure, with municipalities lowering
debt ratios at the expense of investment. However, Section 4.5 shows that ﬁscal consolidation for
the general government was less pronounced than assumed in the baseline scenario. Overall, the
ﬁndings do not support a signiﬁcant impact of the debt brake on municipal investment activity.

Slope eﬀects during rule enforcement (2011–2019). Once the rule is in place, annual

diﬀerentials document the debt brake’s core eﬀect: steady deleveraging rather than abrupt

at roughly triple the rate. This result suggests that, had Germany’s primary balance not jumped
sharply immediately after the introduction of the debt brake, it would likely have risen more strongly

per year more than that of its synthetic control, indicating substantial reliance on the exceptional
borrowing provision. In contrast, the general government debt resumes its pre-crisis downward
trajectory, suggesting that state and local governments largely refrained from comparable credit
expansions. The synthetic control follows a similar path, resulting in a statistically insigniﬁcant

by 0.83 and 0.85 percentage points per year, respectively. The primary balance, after a steep

method yields reliable estimates across all outcome variables, as neither the intercepts nor the
baseline trends diﬀer signiﬁcantly from those of the treated unit. Second, the debt brake appears
to have fulﬁlled its primary objective: achieving a sustained and statistically signiﬁcant reduction
in public debt during non-crisis periods. Third, the results suggest that, in the absence of the debt
brake, the general government investment ratio may have been higher during the 2011–2019 period.
Combined with the comparatively stronger decline in the general government debt ratio, this ﬁnding
may reﬂect vertical dynamics between diﬀerent levels of government. While this is likely driven by the
mechanisms discussed in Section 4.3, the role of vertical ﬁscal interactions merits closer investigation.

Furthermore, the question remains whether Germany’s low debt level has led to lower risk premiums
and, consequently, to reduced ﬁnancing costs. The preceding results suggest that the ﬁnancial crisis
initially had a decisive impact on the level of 10-year yields on German federal bonds. Although
an increase in the eﬀect can be observed following the introduction, the eﬀect cannot be clearly
attributed to the debt brake.19 Nonetheless, there is well-documented evidence of the relationship
between a declining debt ratio and decreasing reﬁnancing costs (Reinhart and Rogoﬀ, 2010; Kumar
and Baldacci, 2010; Afonso et al., 2015). Therefore, we additionally calculate joint weights for the
debt ratio and the 10-year bond yield.20
Finally, we construct joint weights for the general governments’ primary balance and investment
ratio. This approach allows us to assess whether an increasing (decreasing) primary balance is
associated with a simultaneous increase (decrease) in the investment ratio. A positive primary
balance suggests that a government can service its debt from its revenues excluding interest
payments. On the other hand, the government investment ratio represents the proportion of
public expenditure allocated to investments. Moreover, the CITS approach in Subsection 4.4
suggests a potential relationship between the primary balance and the investment ratio at the
general government level. Therefore, analyzing both the primary balance and the investment ratio
together helps in understanding the trade-oﬀs and synergies between ﬁscal sustainability and growth
objectives.

side eﬀects for government bonds. Two key drivers of demand for sovereign bonds are the safety
and liquidity attributes, i.e. the ‘Convenience Yield’ (Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen, 2012).
There is little doubt that these factors have exerted a signiﬁcant inﬂuence on the level of (German)
bond yields (also compared to other safe assets), particularly since the onset of the ﬁnancial crisis,
when there has been an increased demand for risk-free assets with high liquidity (Grandia et al.,
2019) and an increased demand by purchases of the central bank (Ferdinandusse et al., 2017).
Ultimately, investors are willing to accept low (or even negative) bond yields in exchange for these
attributes. A second driver of this development has been the scarcity of German government bonds
compared to other sovereign safe assets, resulting from a reduction (or an expectation thereof) in
market supply due to German ﬁscal policy and large-scale asset purchases by the ECB (Paret and
Weber, 2019; Billio et al., 2025).

Literature suggests that the gap emerging after 2012 may be related to Mario Draghi’s July
2012 statement that the ECB was prepared to do ‘whatever it takes’ to preserve the Euro. This
declaration culminated in the launch of the Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT) program in
September 2012. Although, as of November 2024, no sovereign bond purchases have been made
under the OMT, it is believed that the mere announcement had a calming and yield-lowering eﬀect
on ﬁnancial markets. However, there is little evidence to suggest that this announcement had a more
pronounced impact on Germany than on other EMU countries (Altavilla et al., 2014; Rieth et al.,
2016; Jäger and Grigoriadis, 2017).
Moreover, the synthetic control is composed predominantly of non-EMU countries, with Norway
contributing the largest share (33%) and Luxembourg being the most heavily weighted EMU
member (15%) (see Figure 21 in Appendix I). This composition mitigates potential spillover eﬀects
within the euro area, such as Germany’s role as a “safety anchor.” Empirical studies suggest limited
transmission of the ECB’s unconventional monetary policy to non-EMU countries. Fratzscher et al.
(2016) and Georgiadis and Gräb (2016) ﬁnd modest or negligible eﬀects on global bond yields,
while Korus (2019) reports negative eﬀects on yields only in selected non-EMU countries (Denmark,
Sweden, and Norway).
Taken together, although our approach does not allow deﬁnitive causal attribution due to
confounding events, the observed divergence in bond yields, the limited exposure of the control
group to ECB interventions, and existing evidence on the role of ﬁscal fundamentals in determining
yields support the plausibility of a debt brake eﬀect on long-term reﬁnancing costs.

5
Robustness Checks
In addition to the inference procedures already conducted and the CITS approach, we employ
further robustness checks to assess the validity of the results.
Compliance with ﬁscal rules. A prerequisite for applying the SCM is that the control states,
which make up the synthetic control unit, have not been treated or at least not to the same extent
as the treated unit. In fact, nearly every state in the OECD, from which the control states are
selected, has implemented a ﬁscal rule. However, the more relevant question is not whether a state
has introduced a ﬁscal rule, but whether it has been eﬀectively implemented.
Using the European Union’s compliance tracker, we exclude states based on whether their ﬁscal
policy was in line with the Maastricht criteria. Speciﬁcally, we exclude states (between 2011 and
2022) with either a higher compliance rate than Germany or a compliance rate above 50%. This
procedure does not substantially change the baseline results (see Appendix II. A relatively distinct
eﬀect is observed, particularly for variables where a direct inﬂuence of higher compliance can be
39

==== page 43 ====
expected. The ATT on the federal government’s debt ratio is most pronounced in the third scenario,
while only a slight negative eﬀect is detected for the general government. With respect to the primary
balance, the ATT increases as compliance decreases within the control group. In contrast, no clear
eﬀect is observed for the 10-year bond yield. However, the direction of the eﬀect remains consistent.
For investment ratios, almost no eﬀect is observed, with higher compliance to ﬁscal rules having
only a minor inﬂuence.
Overall, we consider our results to be robust and valid based on this analysis, even though ﬁscal
rules are present within the control group. For more details, see Appendix II. Furthermore, Abadie
et al. (2015) and Abadie (2021) propose two robustness checks to ensure the credibility of the results.
We follow their recommendations and conduct (1) a backdating and (2) a Leave-One-Out (LOO)
procedure.
Backdated estimates. The in-time placebo study reassigns the treatment to earlier years (2008
and 2009) to account for potential preemptive reactions and endogeneity concerns. This procedure
helps distinguish genuine intervention eﬀects from those driven by prior expectations or confounding
events. In the absence of such eﬀects prior to the intervention, the synthetic control method (SCM)
should be able to reproduce the trajectory of the outcome variable. If the trajectory of the outcome
variable changes signiﬁcantly, one should be careful to attribute observable eﬀects only to the
intervention.
The backdating analysis reveals no substantial diﬀerences in the trajectories of the synthetic
control across the majority of variables relative to the ‘original’ synthetic control. For most of the
variables, the results from the backdating procedure are reasonably close to the baseline estimates
(see Subsection 4.2). More important, however, is the eﬀect of this crisis on long-term interest rates
(i.e., the 10-year bond yield). Upon visual inspection of Figure 1 and Figure 4, a treatment eﬀect
does not only become apparent in the year of the introduction of the debt brake, but already in
2008.
This eﬀect arises regardless of the chosen treatment time point. However, the estimates for 2008
and 2009 result in synthetic control trajectories that lie below the original 2011 estimate. We believe
this is due to the safety and liquidity premia of German government bonds following the ﬁnancial
and Euro crisis. This assessment is strengthened by the following Leave-One-Out analysis. For more
details, see Appendix III.
Leave-One-Out estimates. To test the sensitivity of the ATT to changes in country weights,
Abadie et al. (2015) suggests a leave-one-out (LOO) procedure, where countries with positive weights
in the baseline model are iteratively excluded. The LOO approach serves as a robustness check to
evaluate the sensitivity and reliability of the SCM estimates by systematically excluding each control
unit from the donor pool, one at a time, to reconstruct the synthetic control without the excluded
unit. This iterative process assesses how the omission of any single control unit inﬂuences the
trajectory of the outcome variable. If excluding a unit signiﬁcantly alters the results without aﬀecting
40

==== page 44 ====
the preintervention ﬁt, it suggests that the estimate’s change may stem from other interventions or
substantial unique and exogenous shocks to the excluded units’ outcome.22
The results show slight changes in the trajectories and ATT, with notable deviations for
investment ratios, particularly due to Belgium’s signiﬁcant weight (see Figure 18). For the 10-year
bond yield, a ﬁrst gap emerges in 2009, likely due to the ﬁnancial crisis (Afonso et al., 2015), and a
second in 2012, potentially linked to the European sovereign debt crisis and the ECB’s ‘whatever it
takes’ announcement. However, while markets may have required time to assess the impact of the
debt brake (Leeper, 2010; Iara and Wolﬀ, 2014), we cannot clearly attribute the second gap to this
policy measure. For more details, see Appendix IV.
6
Conclusion
This study provides robust empirical evidence on the eﬀects of the German debt brake on ﬁscal
consolidation, debt sustainability, and ﬁnancing costs. Our ﬁndings suggest that the debt brake
played a central role in stabilizing German public ﬁnances throughout the 2010s. While favorable
macroeconomic conditions also contributed to ﬁscal improvement, they were not the primary driver
of debt reduction. Instead, the introduction of stringent ﬁscal rules signiﬁcantly curtailed government
debt accumulation and improved overall ﬁscal discipline.
Our baseline results conﬁrm these ﬁndings across multiple ﬁscal indicators. The debt ratio
at both the general and federal government levels exhibited a signiﬁcant decline following the
implementation of the debt brake. The SCM estimates indicate that without the debt brake,
the federal government’s debt ratio would have continued its upward trajectory, while the actual
ﬁgures demonstrated a sustained reduction. Similarly, the general government debt ratio showed
a notable divergence from its counterfactual, reinforcing the eﬀectiveness of the ﬁscal rule in
curbing debt accumulation. Importantly, the baseline results suggest pronounced vertical eﬀects
in debt accumulation: while the federal government successfully reduced its debt levels, the general
government debt ratio declined even more substantially, indicating a broader ﬁscal consolidation
across multiple tiers of government. An analysis using multiple outcome weights supports this ﬁnding
as well, although the eﬀect appears less pronounced.
With regard to public investment, the results are mixed. At the federal level, the debt brake had
no statistically signiﬁcant eﬀect on the investment ratio, suggesting that federal public investment
was not constrained. At the general government level, by contrast, the investment ratio experienced
a temporary decline, which may reﬂect adjustment by subnational governments in response to ﬁscal
constraints. However, theoretical considerations, inconclusive inference results, and only moderately
pronounced vertical eﬀects in debt accumulation suggest that this decline is unlikely to be causally
linked to the debt brake. Nevertheless, these ﬁndings underscore the importance of accounting for

The primary balance results further support the eﬀectiveness of the debt brake. Our
estimates reveal a clear increase in the primary balance following the implementation of the rule,
demonstrating its role in fostering ﬁscal discipline. This eﬀect persisted until the activation of the
exemption clause in 2020, when expansionary ﬁscal policies led to a reversal of this trend.
The debt brake also had implications for ﬁnancing costs. Our analysis shows that the 10-year
government bond yields decreased more signiﬁcantly in Germany than in its synthetic counterpart,
indicating that investors responded positively to the enhanced ﬁscal discipline. Although other
macroeconomic factors may have contributed to this decline, the evidence suggests that the debt
brake played a role in reducing sovereign borrowing costs.
Despite these ﬁndings, our study has certain limitations. First, while we employed rigorous
methods to estimate the causal eﬀects of the debt brake, endogeneity cannot be entirely ruled out.
The observed ﬁscal discipline may partly stem from pre-existing government preferences rather
than solely from the debt brake itself. Still, preferences do not translate into ﬁscal outcomes
without institutional instruments. The ﬁscal-policy shift following the 2025 reform of the debt
brake is consistent with this view: to create legal room for substantially higher borrowing, the ﬁscal
framework ﬁrst had to be loosened. This suggests that the debt brake had previously operated
as a binding institutional constraint, even if this ex-post development cannot by itself establish
causality. Second, potential spillover eﬀects on other OECD countries remain an open question.
Given Germany’s economic signiﬁcance, its adoption of strict ﬁscal rules may have indirectly
inﬂuenced ﬁscal behavior in other nations, making it diﬃcult to isolate the treatment eﬀect. Lastly,
many of the control states in our analysis also implemented ﬁscal rules, albeit with varying degrees
of strictness.
The ﬁndings suggest that well-designed and credibly enforced ﬁscal rules are eﬀective tools
for promoting long-term ﬁscal sustainability. Despite some remaining concerns, empirical evidence
largely supports the view that such rules reduce public debt, likely lower sovereign risk, and do
not aﬀect public investment. Future research should further explore their eﬀects across diﬀerent
institutional settings, especially given evolving economic challenges and shifting ﬁscal priorities.
Particular attention should be paid to vertical eﬀects, such as variations depending on state structure
or the ﬁscal autonomy of subnational entities.

Appendix II
As mentioned above, a fundamental prerequisite for applying the SCM is that the countries
comprising the synthetic control are not aﬀected by the treatment, or at least not to the same
extent. However, almost every country within the OECD has introduced some form of ﬁscal rule
(see Figure 14). Nevertheless, the crucial factor is not the mere existence of a ﬁscal rule but its
quality and whether it is actually enforced in practice. In our case, we need to examine whether,
since the introduction of the debt brake in 2011, the control countries were subject to a ﬁscal
rule and, more importantly, whether it was adhered to. For this purpose, we utilize the European
Union’s compliance tracker, which assesses the extent to which the Maastricht criteria (which are
relatively weak compared to the German debt brake) were complied with (see also Larch et al.,
2023). Consequently, the control group includes only EU member states and those that, on average,
have shown lower compliance than Germany.23 We rely on this index rather than the Fiscal Rules
Dataset of the IMF (see Davoodi et al., 2022b).because the latter evaluates de jure ﬁscal rules but
does not take eﬀective enforcement into account. As a result, the de jure ﬁscal rule index can be
misleading (Dermine and Larch, 2023). Descriptively, it can be observed that the de jure stringency
of a ﬁscal rule does not necessarily lead to lower debt ratios (see Figure 24).

The second robustness check suggested by Abadie et al. (2015) is the ‘in-time placebo’ study, where
the treatment is reassigned to a diﬀerent point in time other than 2011. If there was a large diﬀerence
to the original estimate, this would indicate a lack of predictive power rather than an actual eﬀect
due to the intervention. Instead of picking a random year in the preintervention period, we chose to
investigate two speciﬁc dates. First, if political actors anticipated the introduction of the debt brake
a priori, the estimates may be biased. The debt brake was already decided at the beginning of 2009;
political actors probably knew by the end of 2008 that such a ﬁscal rule would be implemented. For
the federal states, possible anticipatory eﬀects are even more pronounced, since at the state level
the prohibition of a structural deﬁcit has only been in full eﬀect since 2020. To capture the full
extent of the debt brake, the intervention is backdated to a point before anticipatory eﬀects can be
expected. The temporal variation of the outcome variable is not distorted by this approach, even if
the backdating occurs too early.
Thus, to address anticipatory eﬀects, we run the baseline estimate with the intervention in 2011
also for the year 2009. Additionally, in 2009, there was a change in the federal government, with the
conservative and liberal parties forming the governing coalition, which, in contrast to the previous
coalition of the conservative and social democratic parties, was fully supportive of a stricter ﬁscal
rule. We thereby also address endogeneity issues. The second backdating point is the global ﬁnancial
crisis, beginning in 2007/2008, reaching its full extent in 2008. This crisis has led to huge surges of
government debt and spending alike.
The backdating analysis reveals no substantial diﬀerences in the trajectories of the synthetic
control across the majority of variables relative to the ‘original’ synthetic control. For most of the
variables, the backdating procedure produces results reasonably close to the baseline estimates (see
Subsection 4.2).
More importantly, however, is the eﬀect of this crisis on long-term interest rates (i.e., the 10-year
bond yield). Upon visual inspection of Figure 1 and Figure 4, a treatment eﬀect becomes apparent
not only in the year of the introduction of the debt brake, but already in 2008. This eﬀect arises
regardless of the chosen treatment time point. However, the estimates for 2008 and 2009 result in
synthetic control trajectories that lie below the original 2011 estimate. We believe this is due to the
safety and liquidity premia of German government bonds following the ﬁnancial and Euro crisis.
This assessment is strengthened by the following Leave-One-Out analysis.

Appendix IV
To control for donor states having an excessive impact on the overall eﬀect, Abadie et al. (2015)
suggests a leave-one-out procedure to test the sensitivity of the ATT to changes in the country
weights w. The baseline model is iteratively reestimated to construct a synthetic control, where in
each iteration a country of the control group that received a positive weight in the baseline model is
excluded. Although excluding countries that have received a positive weight reduces the precision of
the methodology because the pre-treatment ﬁt necessarily worsens, it allows us to assess the extent
to which the results for Germany are inﬂuenced by a speciﬁc country from the original control
group. We do this for each variable of interest, as depicted in Figure 28.
The trajectories of the estimates change slightly, as does the ATT. For the investment ratios,
particularly at the general government level, we report more pronounced changes. This can be
attributed to the signiﬁcant weight of Belgium in the original synthetic control (see Figure 18).
When Belgium is excluded from the control, the ATT increases substantially. However, we consider
these results to be unreliable due to poor pre-treatment ﬁt. Nevertheless, caution should be exercised

in the interpretation due to the signiﬁcant weight attributed to Belgium (which is true for both
SCM and ASCM). For the investment ratio of the federal government, some deviations can also be
observed; however, overall the observations tend to ﬂuctuate around the original value and exhibit
a similar trend.
For the 10-year bond yield, the overall trajectory of the synthetic controls remains comparable.
In all cases, a ﬁrst gap emerges in 2009. Then in 2012, a second gap emerges. After 2013,
the gap increases further, but at a much lower rate. With regard to the second gap and its
subsequent development, two exceptions emerge. First, when Japan is excluded, the pre-treatment
ﬁt deteriorates signiﬁcantly. Furthermore, the gap in 2012 does not widen; instead, it becomes
smaller. In this scenario, the synthetic control consists of 98% of the Netherlands. In the second
case, when the Netherlands are excluded, the synthetic control is composed of 85% of countries
outside the EMU. In this case, a further widening of the gap beyond 2012 can be observed.
The ﬁrst gap suggests that the ﬁnancial crisis led to lower reﬁnancing costs for Germany, even
compared to the Netherlands (Afonso et al., 2015). The second gap may be related to the European
sovereign debt crisis. As it reached its peak, it eventually led the President of the European Central
Bank (ECB) to announce in July 2012 that the ECB would do ‘whatever it takes’ to save the Euro.
However, studies examining the impact of this announcement show little to no eﬀect on the level
of Germany’s bond yields. Although the OMT announcement did not have a signiﬁcant eﬀect on
German bond yields, the premium on these bonds spiked during the European debt crisis 2012/2013
and, despite falling afterwards, remained elevated (Paret and Weber, 2019). The exclusion of Japan
from the analysis and the subsequent ﬂattening of the gap after 2012 aligns with the observation
that the Netherlands, like Germany and in contrast to other countries of the EMU, maintained
a safe haven status (Klose and Weigert, 2014). However, if the debt brake had signiﬁcant eﬀects,
these should have been observable as early as 2011. Yet, it is plausible that the introduction of
the debt brake in 2011 had delayed eﬀects on interest rates. Markets may have required time to
fully assess the implications of the measure, particularly to assess whether the value of government
bonds is consistent with future expectations (Leeper, 2010). Similarly, Iara and Wolﬀ(2014) note
that ‘immediate simultaneity between ﬁscal rules and sovereign spreads can be ruled out because
of the usual adoption lags of political reform.’ However, we cannot clearly attribute the second gap
and its subsequent development to the German debt brake.
